Selah Guide · Supplements

Foundations first.

A Selah supplement guide — an audit before a shopping list. Forms matter, cofactors matter, timing matters. Most supplement content sells; this one sorts.

Read this first — the guardrails are the guide. This is education only. This guide never says “you should take.” It says “people discuss X with their provider when…” — because the right answer depends on your medications, your labs, and your body. Every chapter carries its cautions in the chapter, not in fine print. Two rules hold everywhere: test, don’t guess for iron and vitamin D; and pharmacists are the free interaction experts — before anything new, ask one to check your actual list. High-interaction herbs in the St.-John’s-wort class are excluded from this guide entirely.

Chapter 1

Before you buy — the foundations audit

Most shelves were built one crisis at a time: something for sleep from a hard month, something for energy from a worse one, a recommendation from a podcast, a bottle from a friend. Nobody designed the whole. The audit asks three questions of every bottle: What gap is this meant to fill? Is that gap real — tested, or at least evidenced? And is it filling it? Food first, labs where labs exist, and rhythm before products: an adaptogen on top of a destroyed sleep curve is decoration.

“Test everything; hold fast what is good.” — 1 Thessalonians 5:21. The operating verse of this guide.
Artefact — the shelf audit worksheet

One row per bottle, four columns: what it is · the gap it was for · keep / pause / drop / ask-about · monthly cost. Bring it to appointments. Bring it to the pharmacist. An honest shelf is usually a shorter shelf.

Chapter 2

The foundations

Magnesium. The cofactor in hundreds of reactions, including the ones that make energy and calm. Forms matter: glycinate is the well-absorbed, gentle form most people discuss for sleep and tension; oxide is poorly absorbed and mostly a laxative. Educational ranges commonly discussed run 200–400mg elemental, in the evening. “Working” feels like: less braced at night, easier sleep onset — over two to four weeks, not two days. Vitamin D3 + K2. D is a hormone precursor, not just a vitamin; K2 is its partner for directing calcium. In northern latitudes, deficiency is the default — which is why this one is tested, not guessed (Chapter 6). Doses are set against a blood level, with a provider. Omega-3 (EPA + DHA). The anti-inflammatory fats; fish oil quality varies enormously. Common educational ranges are 1–2g combined EPA+DHA daily, with food. B-complex. Look for active forms (methylfolate, methylcobalamin) if you supplement; food sources are in the Nutrition Guide.

Cautions, in the same breath: high-dose omega-3 can interact with blood thinners — disclose it before any surgery and to any prescriber. Magnesium can reduce absorption of some antibiotics and thyroid medication — separate by several hours. Vitamin D at high doses without testing can push calcium too high. Foundations are gentle, not harmless-by-default.
Chapter 3

Blood sugar support

Myo-inositol is the gentlest tool here — a nutrient-like compound involved in insulin signalling, most studied in PCOS and metabolic contexts, commonly discussed at 2–4g/day. It is generally well tolerated; its main caution is that it works on the same pathway as blood-sugar medication. Berberine is the stronger, more drug-like one: a plant alkaloid with real evidence for glucose and lipid effects, commonly studied at 500mg with meals, two to three times daily — and real interactions to match.

Cautions, before anything else: berberine can add to the effect of diabetes medication (hypoglycemia risk) and affects how the liver processes many drugs. It is not used in pregnancy or while breastfeeding. Inositol alongside diabetes medication needs monitoring for the same additive reason. Neither replaces the protein anchor — they support a curve that food and rhythm are already steadying. If you arrived here from the Check-Up because you already take these: this chapter and the next are your caution pages. Read them before your next dose, not after your next purchase.
Chapter 4

HPA support, honestly

Adaptogens modulate the stress response; they do not replace sleep, food, or a set-down load. Rhythm comes first — that ordering is clinical, not decorative. Ashwagandha (KSM-66 is the studied extract, commonly 300–600mg/day) has the deepest evidence for cortisol and perceived stress. Rhodiola is the more stimulating one, taken in the morning, discussed for fatigue-dominant patterns. Phosphatidylserine is discussed for evening cortisol that won’t come down.

Who should not, and what interacts: ashwagandha can interact with thyroid medication (it may raise thyroid hormone levels), with sedatives, and with immunosuppressants; it is not used in pregnancy, and people with autoimmune conditions discuss it with their provider first — an immune modulator is not neutral there. Rhodiola can be activating and can interact with antidepressants and stimulants. If your shelf already includes adaptogens, the caution pages are Chapters 3–4 — start there.
Chapter 5

Gut support

Probiotics: strains over CFU theatre. A label shouting 50 billion of unnamed strains tells you less than a modest product naming strains studied for your actual pattern. Probiotics are guests, not residents — they work while you take them, alongside the food environment of the gut-brain reset. L-glutamine is the gut lining’s preferred fuel, commonly discussed at 5g/day in water for defined seasons, not forever. Digestive basics — eating slowly, chewing, not eating under acute stress — outperform most capsules and cost nothing.

When gut symptoms need a workup instead: blood in stool, unexplained weight loss, pain that wakes you at night, persistent vomiting, or symptoms that progress despite gentle work. Supplements are not a substitute for investigating red flags. Immunocompromised readers discuss probiotics with their provider first.
Chapter 6

Iron and the mood nutrients

The ferritin story deserves its own chapter because it is where “your labs are normal” most often misleads. Ferritin — stored iron — is reported against a wide reference range; a value can be “in range” and still sit far below what hair follicles, dopamine synthesis, and thyroid conversion actually need. Many integrative providers discuss optimal ranges well above the lab’s floor for symptomatic women. That conversation belongs with your provider and a blood test — because the opposite error is real too: iron supplemented without a test can overload, and iron is not a “more is better” nutrient.

B12 and folate follow the same rule: test, then decide. B12 is low in many people who eat few animal foods, take acid-suppressing medication, or have absorption issues — and its deficiency mimics depression and brain fog convincingly. These are mood nutrients in the most literal sense: the chemistry of mood runs on them.

Artefact — the provider-questions card
  • “Can we check ferritin, B12, folate, and 25-OH vitamin D — and talk about optimal ranges, not just the reference range?”
  • “Given my medications, are there interactions with anything on my shelf list?”
  • “Which of these symptoms would you want to investigate rather than supplement?”
  • “When should we recheck, and what change would we expect to see?”
Chapter 7

Build your shelf

Now apply the audit. A finished shelf is short enough to explain: each bottle has a named gap, a form that absorbs, a time of day, and a review date. Do the monthly cost reckoning honestly — a $180/month shelf that duplicates itself is a stressor wearing a wellness costume, and stress is the thing this whole library exists to reduce.

The Bereans “examined the Scriptures every day to see if what Paul said was true” — and were called noble for checking. Checking is not unbelief. It is the practice. — Acts 17
Artefact — the timing chart
  • With food: omega-3, vitamin D3+K2, B-complex (morning), berberine (with meals, if used).
  • Evening: magnesium glycinate; ashwagandha (if used) often rides with the evening meal.
  • Morning, empty-ish stomach: rhodiola (if used) — it is activating.
  • Iron’s rules: away from thyroid medication by several hours, away from calcium and coffee, with vitamin C to help absorption — and only with a test behind it.
Artefact — the interaction-check routine

Before anything new: pharmacist first (free, fast, and it is literally their expertise), then your prescriber at the next visit, with the shelf audit in hand. No guide — including this one — outranks the people who can see your whole chart.
