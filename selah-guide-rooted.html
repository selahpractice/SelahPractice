Rooted.

What your body learned early, why the protector patterns exist, and the ACT layer: making room for what is, and moving toward what matters anyway.

Chapter 1

What ACEs are

Adverse Childhood Experiences — ACEs — are the repeated stress of a hard childhood. The original research named ten: physical, emotional, and sexual abuse; physical and emotional neglect; a parent's addiction or mental illness; a mother treated violently; losing a parent to separation, divorce, or death; a household member in prison. You do not need the list memorised. You need the mechanism: a child's stress system is built by its environment, and when the environment is chronically unsafe, the system is built for alarm.

The numbers, plainly: about two in three adults carry at least one ACE, and the higher the count, the higher the risk for adult illness — heart disease, autoimmune conditions, depression, chronic pain. That is not a verdict. It is a dose-response curve with a hopeful tail: the same research shows that safe, stable, nurturing relationships — at any age — change the curve. Adversity is not destiny. It is a setting, and settings can be re-taught.

This guide does not ask you to count yours. No score here, no label. If the word fits, it fits; the work is the same either way.

Chapter 2

Complex trauma, not a single event

Single-event trauma has a before and an after — a car crash, a loss, a night you can point to. Complex trauma is different: it is repeated, relational, and without a safe exit. It is the pattern of a childhood, not a single bad day. The people who were supposed to be the safety were also the threat, or were absent when the threat came — so the system never learned what safe feels like in a body, only what to do to survive one.

That is why complex trauma does not heal by "processing the event," the way a single trauma sometimes does. There is no one event to process. There is a way of being that was learned: stay alert, be useful, don't need, don't feel — or feel everything at once. The healing is not a return. It is a first arrival: learning, as an adult, what the system never got to learn as a child. Slowly. In relationship. In the body.

If you have ever been told you are "too sensitive," "too much," or "too broken" — hear this plainly: a system that adapted to an unlivable childhood is not a defective system. It is a faithful one. It did exactly what it was built to do.

Chapter 3

How it shows up in the body

Complex trauma shows up as protectors — the moves your system makes first when it is overwhelmed. Rev up and push (fight). Get busy and outrun it (flight). Go numb and shut down (freeze). Smooth it over and take care of everyone else (fawn). The Regulate guide names each one and gives it a first move; this chapter is about why they exist. A protector is not a character flaw and not a diagnosis. It is physiology with a history — protection that learned its job early and does it faithfully.

It also shows up as the body carrying what the voice couldn't. My own chain, plainly: adverse childhood experiences → complex trauma → a body that learned emotional suppression as safety → psoriasis and thyroid carrying what my voice couldn't. The science behind that chain is psychoneuroimmunology: stress, immunity, and belief speak to one another, and a system that has been bracing for decades will eventually say so in the tissue. That is not your body failing you. It is your body telling the truth, in the only language it was allowed.

And it shows up as shame — the feeling that the problem is you, not what happened to you. Shame is the one protector that hides the wound it guards. Its treatment is the opposite of hiding: being spoken to, witnessed, and not flinched at. The Emotional Contingency Plan has a plan for shame specifically, because shame is processed differently from fear — spoken, not hidden; witnessed, not covered.

Chapter 4

The ACT layer

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy — ACT — is built on one counterintuitive finding: struggling against hard thoughts and feelings often makes them bigger. The alternative is not giving up. It is making room. Selah's own line — we make room; God heals — is the ACT move, in the Bible's language. Acceptance here is not resignation. Resignation says nothing will change. Willingness says: I stop spending my strength on the fight, so I can spend it on what matters.

Three moves, in order. Defusion: thoughts are weather, not verdicts. "I am having the thought that I am too much" is a different sentence from "I am too much" — and a different life. Name the thought, thank the mind for trying to protect you, and return to the room. Values: what matters enough to move toward even while the feeling is present — not a goal, a direction. Ask: what would I do today if this feeling came with me instead of deciding for me? Committed action: one small step toward the value, today, while the feeling is still there. Not after it lifts. With it.

"My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness." — 2 Corinthians 12:9. And: "be transformed by the renewing of your mind." — Romans 12:2

ACT is not a replacement for trauma therapy. It is the layer underneath the doing — the stance that makes the practices possible, and the practices survivable. For the reader whose suffering did not lift, ACT pairs with Frankl's question: not "how do I make this stop," but "what meaning is still available to me, even here?"

Chapter 5

Practices — the early map and the compass

The early map. Two lines, written once: what my system learned early — "stay alert," "be useful," "don't need" — and what I am teaching it now. The second line is the work of this guide, one safe experience at a time. Keep it where a hard day can find it.

The defusion script. When the old thought arrives — "I am too much," "I am unsafe," "no one stays" — say it back in its full form: "I am having the thought that…" Then: "Thank you, mind, for trying to protect me." Then look at the room and name five things you can see. The thought does not have to leave. It has to stop driving.

The values compass. One sentence: what matters most right now. One step: what I will do toward it today, feeling and all. The compass is for the days the feeling is loud — it does not wait for quiet to point north.

The two-minute Selah. Lean back. Breathe out longer than you breathe in. Say it quietly: "There is nothing I need to respond to right now." Stay for two minutes. If a worry knocks, answer it the same way: "Not right now." Then return to the pause. The full practice is the Regulate guide, Chapter 8.

Co-regulation, on purpose. A calm voice, a safe face, a person who is not alarmed by your state — these are measurable physiological inputs. Build your life with at least one person whose presence your body reads as safe, and let yourself be regulated by them on purpose, without apology. Receiving regulation is not weakness. It is how the system was designed to work.

Chapter 6

When to find a therapist

This guide teaches understanding and practice. It does not process trauma — and it should not try. If the past is intruding on the present — flashbacks, nightmares, avoidance that is shrinking your life; if shutdown or revving is costing you work, relationships, or safety; if reading this guide stirred something you do not want to carry alone — that is the guide knowing its edge, and the edge is a doorway.

Ask your provider about: trauma-informed therapy (a therapist who understands complex trauma specifically, not just general counselling); EMDR — a structured processing approach with strong evidence for trauma; IFS — Internal Family Systems, which works with the protector parts this guide names; and somatic experiencing — body-first processing for what words cannot reach. These are things to ask about, not prescriptions. The right fit matters more than the right acronym: a therapist your body reads as safe is the treatment beginning.

Voices worth your time

The science in this guide comes from other people's life's work, and I am a navigator, not the source. Here is where that conversation lives, in its own home.

Dr. Nadine Burke Harris — ACEs and toxic stress — California's first Surgeon General, and the physician who connected childhood adversity to adult illness through the stress system. Her book The Deepest Well and her talk "How Childhood Trauma Affects Health Across a Lifetime" are the clearest doorway into ACEs science.

Pete Walker — Complex PTSD — a psychotherapist who has spent decades with adults traumatised in childhood. Complex PTSD: From Surviving to Thriving is the practical handbook for the repeated, relational kind — the kind this guide is about.

Steven Hayes and the Association for Contextual Behavioral Science — ACT's co-founder and its home. The library, the research, and the training all live here, for when you want the framework from the people who built it.

Russ Harris — The Happiness Trap — the plain-language ACT book, over a million copies: stop struggling against difficult thoughts and feelings, and build a life around what matters. ACT in ordinary words.

Deb Dana — Rhythm of Regulation — the clinician who translated polyvagal theory into everyday language; her site is where states, glimmers, and befriending your own nervous system get a whole library. The companion to this guide is the Regulate guide.
