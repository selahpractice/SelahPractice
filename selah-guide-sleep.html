The Night Shift

A Selah sleep guide — circadian rhythm, the cortisol curve, and sleep that actually restores, in plain language. Every other guide assumes this one is running underneath.

Read this first. This guide is education, not treatment, and it is not a replacement for your own providers. It offers no advice on prescription sleep medication — starting, stopping, or dosing is a conversation for the clinician who prescribed it. If your sleep is fragmented by snoring with pauses, gasping, or morning headaches, skip to Chapter 7: that is a medical question, not a habit question. Chapter 1

Your curve

Cortisol is not a stress hormone gone rogue. It is a daily rhythm: it should surge within the first hour of waking — the cortisol awakening response, your body’s own alarm and ignition — then fall steadily across the day, reaching its lowest point around midnight so melatonin can rise and sleep can deepen. Energy, mood, blood sugar, immune repair, and thyroid conversion all ride on that shape.

Most sleep problems are curve problems wearing pyjamas. A blunted morning rise feels like waking up already behind. An evening rise feels like being tired all day and wired at 11 p.m. Before you change anything, read your own pattern: the Check-Up asked when you fall asleep, when you get up, when you feel most depleted, and how you actually wake. Those four answers sketch your curve more honestly than any single “sleep quality” score.

“In peace I will lie down and sleep, for you alone, LORD, make me dwell in safety.” — Psalm 4:8
Artefact — read your curve

Answer three questions for three ordinary days: When do I wake, and how — dragged, or ready? When does the crash come? When does the second wind arrive? Dragged mornings + a 3 p.m. crash + an 11 p.m. second wind is the inverted curve of Chapter 3. Waking at 3–4 a.m. most nights is Chapter 4. Trouble only at the front end — falling asleep — starts at Chapter 2.

Chapter 2

Night One — the 10 p.m. landing

This is the first step, and it is deliberately small. Not a new life — one landing, repeated. A set time matters more than a perfect routine, because the curve entrains to consistency: your body starts preparing melatonin, temperature drop, and sleep pressure around a landing it can predict.

The landing has five parts. A time — 10 p.m., or the closest honest hour to it, kept within half an hour either way. Light down — dim the house 90 minutes before; light after sunset suppresses melatonin for hours. Screens down — the phone charges outside the bedroom, or at minimum goes dark and face-down across the room. The room — cool, dark, boring. The last meal — finished two to three hours before, so digestion is not competing with sleep onset.

“In vain you rise early and stay up late… for He grants sleep to those He loves.” — Psalm 127:2
Artefact — the Night One checklist (fridge page)
  • 8:00 p.m. — last food. Kitchen closes.
  • 8:30 p.m. — lights dimmed. Overhead lights off; lamps only.
  • 9:00 p.m. — screens away. Phone charging outside the bedroom.
  • 9:30 p.m. — warm shower or bath; cool bedroom waiting.
  • 10:00 p.m. — lights out. Same time tomorrow.
Chapter 3

Turning the curve around

For the inverted pattern — wired at night, crashed by mid-afternoon — the fix is rhythm before products, and it runs in a specific order. Morning light first. Outside in daylight within an hour of waking, 10–20 minutes (longer on grey days). Light is the strongest signal your clock takes; it sets tonight’s melatonin as surely as it lifts this morning’s cortisol. Caffeine curfew second. Caffeine’s half-life is five to six hours; a 3 p.m. coffee is still half present at 9 p.m. Nothing after noon while you rebuild. The evening wind-down third — a repeatable descent, not a collapse into bed from full brightness and full noise.

Give it two weeks before you judge it. The curve did not invert in a weekend, and it does not right itself in one either — but most people feel the morning first: waking gets less like surfacing from deep water.

Before God spoke a word to Elijah, He fed him and let him sleep — twice. “Get up and eat, for the journey is too much for you.” — 1 Kings 19
Artefact — the 7-day rhythm tracker

One row per day, four columns: wake time · morning light (yes/no) · caffeine last taken · waking pattern (slept through / woke at ___). Seven honest rows will show you your curve more clearly than any wearable score.

Artefact — the 10-minute wind-down
  • Minutes 0–3 — warm water: shower, bath, or a warm drink.
  • Minutes 3–6 — tomorrow, parked: write the list, close the book.
  • Minutes 6–9 — slow breathing, longer out than in.
  • Minute 10 — lights out. Same script tomorrow.
Chapter 4

The 3 a.m. waking

Waking between 3 and 4 a.m. is so common it has a physiology, not just a misery. In the second half of the night, cortisol begins its climb toward morning; in a stressed system it can spike early and hard. Blood sugar dips in the long fast can trigger the same alarm — the body releases cortisol and adrenaline to liberate glucose, and you wake up wired and slightly shaky. Add a full bladder and a mind that has been waiting all day for a quiet moment to present its worries, and 3 a.m. becomes the busiest hour of the night.

What helps: a protein-anchored evening meal (Chapter 1 of the Nutrition Guide), the Night One landing so the first half of the night is deep, and — in the night itself — not feeding the alarm. The phone and the clock are the two worst responses: clock math (“if I fall asleep NOW I get three hours…”) is a cortisol generator. If you are awake more than about twenty minutes, get up, keep lights low, sit somewhere boring until sleepiness returns.

Jesus slept in the stern of a boat in a storm his friends were panicking about. Rest, in Scripture, is not the absence of weather. — Mark 4
Artefact — the 3 a.m. card (keep by the bed)
  • Don’t check the time. Don’t touch the phone.
  • Slow breath out — longer out than in, five rounds.
  • Cold water on your face if your mind is racing.
  • Still awake after ~20 minutes? Up, low light, boring chair, until sleepy.
  • Write the looping worry on the pad. It will keep until morning.
Chapter 5

The honest chapters

Naps. A 20-minute nap before 3 p.m. restores alertness without stealing night sleep pressure. Long, late naps do the opposite — you wake groggy from deep sleep and arrive at bedtime unready. Caffeine. Not a villain — a loan. It borrows alertness from later in the day and charges interest at night; mornings only while you rebuild. Alcohol. It sedates, then fragments: sleep after drinking is lighter, more broken, and lighter on the deep stages where repair happens. It is a sleep thief wearing a nightcap. Cannabis. Many people fall asleep faster with it; the evidence points to suppressed REM and rebound vivid dreaming on stopping. Sleep aids. Prescription medication is your prescriber’s territory, not this guide’s — and over-the-counter options, including melatonin, are supplements: forms, timing, and cautions live in the Supplement Guide.

None of this is moralised here. These are architecture facts. What you do with them is yours — but now they are facts you hold, not fog you live in.

Chapter 6

Sleep when life is loud

Shift work. Anchor one sleep block and defend it like a shift: same hours, blackout dark, phone off, household briefed. Light on the commute home is the enemy — sunglasses are a clinical tool here. Children. Partial protocols are still protocols: keep the landing time for yourself even when the night is interrupted; fragmented sleep in a consistent frame beats perfect sleep in chaos. Pain. Position, heat or cold before bed, and timing pain medication with your provider’s guidance — and pain that regularly destroys sleep belongs in Chapter 7’s conversation. A snoring partner. Earplugs and separate blankets are not a relationship failure; untreated apnea (theirs) is a health issue worth naming kindly. Grief. Grief wakes people at 3 a.m. with the person they lost already in the room. That is not a disorder. Keep the frame, expect the season, and let the card by the bed carry the nights.

Chapter 7

When to get checked

Some fragmented sleep is a medical question wearing a habit costume. Sleep apnea flags: loud snoring with pauses or gasping, witnessed apneas, morning headaches, waking unrefreshed despite enough hours, high blood pressure. Apnea is common, underdiagnosed in women, and worth a sleep study — no protocol in this guide substitutes for treating it. Restless legs — an urge to move the legs at rest, worse at night — has real causes, including low iron stores; it deserves a workup, not willpower. Thyroid dysfunction fragments sleep in both directions. And any sleep problem riding alongside low mood, loud snoring, or falling asleep at the wheel goes to a provider promptly — the last one urgently.

Asking for a workup is not failing at the guide. It is the guide working: rhythm first, and medicine where medicine is the answer.
