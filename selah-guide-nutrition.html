Selah Guide · Functional Nutrition

Fed.

A functional nutrition guide for people who are tired of being tired. Food as information — for blood sugar, inflammation, the gut-brain axis, and hormone signalling. Not typical nutrition: no calorie targets, no food fear.

Read this first. This guide is education, not treatment, and nothing here diagnoses a deficiency. There are no weight-loss promises in it, on purpose. If you are living with an active eating disorder, this guide is not for you right now — please work with your own care team and a dietitian who knows that territory. And if you take medication for blood sugar or blood pressure, know that steadier eating changes medication needs: loop your provider in before you change patterns, not after.

Chapter 1

Blood sugar first — the protein anchor

The question is never “is this food good or bad?” It is “what does this do to your curve?” A meal that spikes glucose and crashes it two hours later takes mood, focus, cravings, and cortisol with it. The 3 p.m. crash is rarely a character problem; it is usually lunch, arriving on schedule. And cravings — salt, sugar, the kitchen at 9 p.m. — are biochemistry, not willpower.

The fastest lever is the protein anchor: a palm-sized portion of protein at every meal, eaten before the starch. Meal order measurably flattens the curve — fibre and protein first, starch last. Coffee alone as a first intake starts the day on a spike-and-scavenge pattern; coffee with food is a different substance, metabolically. If your Check-Up said your first intake is coffee alone or nothing until noon, this chapter is your first step.

Manna arrived daily — gathered daily, enough for the day. Not stockpiled, not feared. Daily bread is a rhythm before it is a menu. — Exodus 16
Artefact — the protein-anchor tracker

Three boxes a day, one per meal. A box is earned by a palm-sized protein, eaten early in the meal. Tick honestly for seven days — most people discover their crash days and their box-less days are the same days.

Artefact — the crash-pattern log

When a crash or craving hits, note three things: the time · what the last meal actually was · what helped. A week of logs turns “I have no energy” into a pattern you can intervene on.

Chapter 2

The Selah plate

Build a plate without weighing anything. Protein anchor — a palm to a palm-and-a-half. Colour range — two or more colours of vegetables; colour is information, the polyphenols your microbiome and immune system read. Fats that help — olive oil, fatty fish, nuts, seeds: the raw materials of hormones and cell membranes. Starch last and honest — present, enjoyed, sequenced. Timing that steadies — three real meals beat a day of grazing and one large landing at night, for most depleted systems.

This is a pattern, not a purity test. A plate that mostly looks like this, most days, moves inflammation, blood sugar, and sleep in the right direction — which is why the guide measures weeks, not meals.

The feast is God’s idea before it is our failure. Food arrives in Scripture as gift, covenant, and celebration far more often than as threat.
Artefact — the Selah plate builder
  • Anchor: palm-sized protein — eggs, fish, chicken, lentils, Greek yogurt, tofu.
  • Colour: two vegetables, two colours.
  • Fat: a thumb-to-two of olive oil, butter, nuts, or seeds.
  • Starch: after the protein has started — rice, potato, bread, enjoyed.
  • Something alive: a fermented food when you can (Chapter 4).
Artefact — grocery list by section

Protein: eggs, tinned fish, chicken thighs, lentils, Greek yogurt. Produce: leafy greens, frozen berries, whatever colour is cheap this week. Pantry: olive oil, oats, tinned tomatoes, spices. Fermented: kefir, sauerkraut, kimchi, live yogurt. Buy the pattern, not the brand names.

Chapter 3

Inflammation, honestly

Inflammation is not an enemy; it is a response — the immune system answering a signal. The problem is a signal that never stops: blood-sugar spikes all day, sleep that never restores, stress hormones that never fall, ultra-processed food as the dietary default. What feeds it is mostly pattern: refined sugar in liquid form, industrial seed oils in everything, alcohol as a nightly ritual, and too little of what cools it — omega-3 fats, fibre, polyphenol colour, sleep.

What cools it is also pattern, not purity. You do not need to be perfect; you need the average to move. Sugar and seed oils without the panic: reduce the defaults, keep the joy. An anti-inflammatory pattern held at 80% for a season will do more than a flawless fortnight followed by collapse and shame.

Chapter 4

The gut-brain reset

Your gut is a nervous system — hundreds of millions of neurons, in constant conversation with the brain through the vagus nerve, producing much of the body’s serotonin and reading every stress hormone you make. Bloating, reflux, irregular bowels, and foods that reliably set you off are not side notes. They are the conversation, audible.

The reset is gentle and two weeks long, built on one rule: crowd out before you cut out. Add first — fermented foods, fibre range, slow meals, chewing (digestion begins with enzymes and time, not supplements). Then notice: which foods reliably cost you? Trial removing the loudest one for the second week and watch. Eating rhythm matters too: the vagus nerve reads meals eaten in a rush, standing, scrolling, as meals eaten under threat.

Artefact — the two-week gentle start
  • Days 1–3: one fermented food daily. Slow one meal a day down to twenty minutes.
  • Days 4–7: add a new fibre source; protein anchor at breakfast.
  • Week 2: trial removing your loudest trigger food. Keep the additions.
  • Throughout: the crash-pattern log from Chapter 1, with a gut column added.

Persistent gut symptoms — blood, unexplained weight loss, pain that wakes you, symptoms that march on despite gentle work — need a workup, not a protocol. The Supplement Guide’s gut chapter covers forms and cautions for gut support, and when to stop supplementing and start investigating.

Chapter 5

Nutrients through food first

The mood-and-energy nutrients have addresses in food. Iron — red meat, lentils, spinach with vitamin C alongside (absorption is a team sport). B12 — animal foods; if you eat few of them, B12 is a test-and-supplement conversation, not a food fix. Folate — leafy greens, legumes, eggs. Vitamin D — sunlight and fatty fish, and in a Winnipeg winter, honesty: food alone rarely gets there, which is what testing is for. Omega-3s — fatty fish twice a week is the food-first benchmark.

When food isn’t enough — a tested deficiency, an absorption problem, a season of real depletion — that is the Supplement Guide’s territory, and it starts with an audit and a test, not a shopping cart.

God fed Elijah with ravens, then with a widow’s last flour, then with an angel’s bread and water — provision arriving in forms nobody would have ordered. — 1 Kings 17 & 19
Chapter 6

Eating in a real life

Budget. The pattern is cheap at its core: eggs, lentils, tinned fish, frozen vegetables, oats. Frozen colour counts. Family tables. One meal, sequenced — you do not need a separate menu; you need the protein anchor and the order, and both hide inside ordinary food. Community meals. Potlucks, church suppers, your mother’s cooking: eat, receive, enjoy. A pattern is measured in weeks, and belonging is also a nutrient — isolation raises inflammatory markers as surely as sugar does. When you’re the one who cooks for everyone — the depleted cook is this guide’s first reader. The anchor applies to the hand that serves, too. Sit down. Eat first bites sitting, not standing at the counter.

Food is gift, not threat. A guide that makes you afraid of your own table has failed, whatever it does to your biomarkers.
