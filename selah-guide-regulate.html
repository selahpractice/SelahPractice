Regulate.

A nervous system field guide, organised by state, not by symptom. STILL is the grounding guide; this is its sequel — state-shifting and capacity-building for the days after the pause.

Read this first. This guide is education and practice, not trauma therapy — and it says so plainly: these practices stop where trauma processing begins. If your history is asking for processing, or your symptoms are escalating, the right next step is a therapist, and Chapter 5 names how to think about that. If you are in crisis, call or text 988 (Canada & US) — and the Crisis Check-In is built for the surge itself. No practice here is a cure for PTSD, anxiety disorders, or depression, and none is framed as one.

Chapter 1

Know your states — name your protector

Polyvagal theory, in plain language: your nervous system is always asking one question — am I safe? — and it answers in states. Ventral is the state of safety and connection: you can think, digest, laugh, pray, be met. Fight/flight is mobilisation: the system revs to meet a threat — pushing, fixing, outrunning. Shutdown is the oldest protection: when revving hasn’t worked or isn’t possible, the system powers down — numb, flat, foggy, gone.

Your protector is the move your system makes first when it’s overwhelmed: rev up and push (fight), get busy and outrun it (flight), go numb (freeze), or smooth it over and take care of everyone else (fawn). The Check-Up asked you yours. A protector is not a character flaw and not a diagnosis — it is physiology with a history, protection that learned its job early and does it faithfully. Naming it is the first move because an unnamed state drives; a named state can be answered.

God breathed into the first human the breath of life — ruach: breath, wind, Spirit, one word. The nervous system’s fastest handle has been in the text since the second chapter. — Genesis 2:7
Artefact — the state-map worksheet

Three rows: Revved · Shutdown · Steady. For each, one line: my signals (what my body does first) and my first move (from Chapters 2–3). Keep it where a hard day can find it — the map is for the moment you can’t think, drawn by the version of you who could.

Chapter 2

For the revved states

Fight and flight are energy looking for somewhere to go. Arguing with the rev rarely works; discharge, then downshift does. Discharge is physical: brisk walking, stairs, shaking it out, cold water on the face or a cold pack at the neck (the dive reflex is a fast vagal brake). Then downshift: breath with a long exhale — out longer than in, which is the exhale’s whole job in the system — and orienting: slowly turning the head and letting the eyes land on real things in the room, telling the threat system, in its own language, that the room is safe.

The first 90 seconds matter most. A stress chemical surge crests and begins to clear in about that long if you don’t re-fuel it with the story. Move first; interpret later.

Artefact — the 60-second resets
  • Cold: cold water on the face, or hold something frozen. Count to thirty.
  • Exhale: in for four, out for eight, five rounds.
  • Orient: name five things you can see, slowly, turning your head.
  • Move: sixty seconds of stairs, marching, or shaking out your hands.
Chapter 3

For shutdown — coming back gently

Shutdown is not laziness and not peace. It is the system conserving: flat affect, fog, the sense of being behind glass. The way back is gentle and in order — warmth before movement, rhythm before effort. Warmth: a warm drink held in both hands, a blanket, a shower. Rhythm: rocking, swaying, a slow walk, music with a steady beat. Voice: humming, singing, reading aloud — the vagus nerve runs through the throat, and using your voice is a physiological input, not a metaphor. Small movement, then people: co-regulation (Chapter 6) is often the fastest door out.

Why “push through it” backfires: shutdown is a threat response. Demanding performance from a system in shutdown confirms the threat and deepens the power-down. Gentleness is not indulgence here — it is the correct signal.

“Be still, and know that I am God.” The stillness of Psalm 46:10 is not striving’s opposite. It is the posture of a system that has stopped bracing.
Artefact — the coming-back menu

Write yours before you need it, three lines: one warmth · one rhythm · one voice. Mine might be: tea in both hands · a slow lap of the block · humming while the kettle boils. Yours will be yours. The menu exists because shutdown is precisely when choosing feels impossible.

Chapter 4

For fawn

Fawn is the protector that looks like kindness: smoothing it over, managing everyone’s feelings, taking care of the room before the room can become a threat. It is a stress state with excellent public relations. Underneath it, the system is braced — scanning, appeasing, spending capacity it does not have — and the bill arrives later as exhaustion, resentment, and the strange grief of being surrounded by people you’ve cared for and feeling entirely alone.

The practice is small and specific: pause before the fix. One breath between the tension and your rescue. Notice the body’s lurch toward smoothing. Then, when you can, one honest sentence instead of the polished one. Fawn’s full treatment lives where it belongs — in the Boundaries & Attachment Guide, especially the yes audit (Chapter 1) and the scripts (Chapter 4). This chapter is the bridge: it names fawn as physiology so the boundary work can be physiology too, not a personality overhaul.

Chapter 5

Building capacity

The window of tolerance is the band where you can feel things and still think, be triggered and still choose. Capacity-building is widening that window — and the method is titration: small doses of challenge, on purpose, with recovery after. Not flooding yourself to “get stronger.” A hard conversation survived with your practices running widens the window a fraction. So does a regulated response to a small trigger, noticed and named.

Track what actually widens yours over a month — not what should. Sleep underneath everything (the Sleep Guide), food that steadies (the Nutrition Guide), and one daily regulation practice chosen from your own map. Capacity is built in ordinary days, quietly.

When to find a therapist: when the past is intruding on the present — flashbacks, nightmares, avoidance that is shrinking your life; when shutdown or revving is costing you work, relationships, or safety; when you want to process, not just cope. That is not this guide failing. That is the guide knowing its edge.

Artefact — the regulation menu card (fridge-ready)
  • Revved → cold, exhale, orient, move (Chapter 2).
  • Shutdown → warmth, rhythm, voice, one person (Chapter 3).
  • Fawn → pause before the fix (Chapter 4).
  • Steady → notice, and thank the system. Steadiness noticed is steadiness reinforced.
Artefact — the weekly capacity tracker

One line a week: my window felt… (narrower / same / wider) · what widened it · what narrowed it. Four lines a month is a clinical picture of your own life.

Chapter 6

Regulation together

Nervous systems regulate each other. A calm voice, a safe face, a person who is not alarmed by your state — these are measurable physiological inputs; co-regulation is the mechanism underneath “being met.” It is why the lonely body heals slower and why worship — singing together, breathing together, rhythm and voice in a room — is a nervous-system practice whether or not anyone calls it one.

This is the Selah thesis made practical: healing is God’s work, and being met is one of the ordinary means He uses. Build your life with at least one person whose presence your body reads as safe — and let yourself be regulated by them on purpose, without apology. Receiving regulation is not weakness. It is how the system was designed to work.

Jesus withdrew to lonely places — and also let Himself be met: fed by friends, anointed, accompanied. Regulation, in His pattern, ran in both directions. — Luke 5:16
Chapter 7

What your body learned early

Adverse Childhood Experiences — ACEs — are the repeated stress of a hard childhood: abuse or neglect, a parent's addiction or mental illness, violence at home, losing a parent. The research is blunt and hopeful at once: about two in three adults carry at least one, and the more there are, the higher the risk for adult illness — not because the body is broken, but because the stress system learned its settings early and never got the all-clear. A body that grew up bracing does not stop bracing on its eighteenth birthday.

Complex trauma is what happens when the adversity is repeated, relational, and without a safe exit — the pattern of a childhood, not a single bad day. It differs from single-event trauma in one decisive way: there is no "before" to return to. The alarm is the baseline. That is why the protector patterns in Chapter 1 exist. They were learned young, and they did their job faithfully: revving when the room was unsafe, shutting down when revving could not help, smoothing it over when the adults needed managing.

None of this is a life sentence. What was learned can be unlearned — slowly, in relationship, in the body. Not by force and not by insight alone. A system that learned to brace can learn to rest, one safe experience at a time. The full telling lives in the Rooted guide; this chapter is the bridge: your protector has a history, and the history has a name.

Artefact — the early map

Two lines, written once: what my system learned early — "stay alert," "be useful," "don't need" — and what I am teaching it now. The second line is the work of this guide, one safe experience at a time.

Chapter 8

The ACT layer — making room, not giving up

Acceptance and Commitment Therapy — ACT — is built on one counterintuitive finding: struggling against hard thoughts and feelings often makes them bigger. The alternative is not giving up. It is making room. Selah's own line — we make room; God heals — is the ACT move, in the Bible's language. Acceptance here is not resignation. Resignation says nothing will change. Willingness says: I stop spending my strength on the fight, so I can spend it on what matters.

Three moves, in order. Defusion: thoughts are weather, not verdicts. "I am having the thought that I am too much" is a different sentence from "I am too much" — and a different life. The Emotion Check-In already teaches this: name the thought, thank the mind for trying to protect you, and return to the room. Values: what matters enough to move toward even while the feeling is present — not a goal, a direction. Ask: what would I do today if this feeling came with me instead of deciding for me? Committed action: one small step toward the value, today, while the feeling is still there. Not after it lifts. With it.

"My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness." — 2 Corinthians 12:9. And: "be transformed by the renewing of your mind." — Romans 12:2
Artefact — the values compass

One sentence: what matters most right now. One step: what I will do toward it today, feeling and all. Write both on a card. The compass is for the days the feeling is loud — it does not wait for quiet to point north.

Voices worth your time

The states in this guide come from polyvagal theory, and I am a navigator, not the source. Here is where that conversation lives, in its own home.

Deb Dana — Rhythm of Regulation — the clinician who translated polyvagal theory into everyday language; her site is where states, glimmers, and befriending your own nervous system get a whole library.

The Polyvagal Institute — the founding home of polyvagal theory, built around Stephen Porges' work — with a free learning library for when you want to hear it from the source itself.

Viktor Frankl — logotherapy — the psychiatrist who survived the camps and came out saying the last human freedom is choosing your attitude toward what cannot be changed. Man's Search for Meaning is the book for the reader whose suffering did not lift — meaning is the ground that remains.

Søren Kierkegaard — the Dane who named despair "the sickness unto death" and anxiety "the dizziness of freedom," and who wrote that life can only be understood backwards, but must be lived forwards. Start with The Sickness Unto Death and The Concept of Anxiety — or the Stanford Encyclopedia entry linked here, which maps the whole terrain.

Dr. Nadine Burke Harris — ACEs and toxic stress — California's first Surgeon General, and the physician who connected childhood adversity to adult illness through the stress system. Her book The Deepest Well and her talk "How Childhood Trauma Affects Health Across a Lifetime" are the clearest doorway into ACEs science.

Pete Walker — Complex PTSD — a psychotherapist who has spent decades with adults traumatised in childhood. Complex PTSD: From Surviving to Thriving is the practical handbook for the repeated, relational kind — the kind this guide's Chapter 7 is about.

Steven Hayes and the Association for Contextual Behavioral Science — ACT's co-founder and its home. The library, the research, and the training all live here, for when you want the framework from the people who built it.

Russ Harris — The Happiness Trap — the plain-language ACT book, over a million copies: stop struggling against difficult thoughts and feelings, and build a life around what matters. ACT in ordinary words.
