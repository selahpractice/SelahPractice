Selah Guide · Boundaries & Attachment

Yours and mine.

A boundaries & attachment guide — boundaries as nervous-system protection, and the patterns underneath them: how you reach, how you hide. Patterns are named without shame here; a pattern is information, not an identity.

Read this first. This guide is education, not therapy, and its attachment language is descriptive, never diagnostic — it names patterns you can watch in yourself, not labels to wear or to hand to other people. And one thing must be said at the top, not the bottom: boundaries are not safety plans. If a relationship involves coercion, control, threats, or violence, a well-phrased boundary is not the tool — real-world help is. In Canada, shelters and support can be found through local crisis lines, and 988 is there around the clock. If that paragraph is about you, start there, not at Chapter 1.

Chapter 1

The yes audit

Start with data, not resolve. For seven days, catch every yes you say and sort it into two columns: given freely — you had the capacity and the want — and given from reflex — the yes that left your mouth before your body was consulted. Most people doing this for the first time are startled less by the count than by the speed: the reflex yes fires in under a second, which tells you it was never a decision. It was a protector (the fawn response, in the Regulation Guide) doing its job.

The audit is not a shaming exercise and it changes nothing by force. It simply moves the yes from reflex to record — and what is recorded can, eventually, be chosen.

Artefact — the 7-day yes-audit worksheet
  • Columns: the ask · my yes · freely / reflex · what it cost (time, energy, sleep, money).
  • Rule: no fixing during the seven days. Just record.
  • Day 7: circle the three costliest reflex yeses. Chapter 3 starts there.
Chapter 2

What a boundary is — and isn’t

A boundary is not a wall, a punishment, or a speech. It is the line where your load ends and someone else’s begins — and Scripture draws exactly that line. Galatians 6 holds both halves in one passage: “carry each other’s burdens” — the crushing weights no one should bear alone — and “each one should carry their own load” — the daily pack that is simply, legitimately, yours. Confusing the two is where exhaustion breeds: treating everyone’s daily load as your burden to carry, and your own crushing burdens as loads you should manage silently.

So a boundary is load-keeping: I will carry what is mine, help carry what is crushing, and stop absorbing what is neither. It is also, physiologically, nervous-system protection — every absorbed load is paid for in cortisol, sleep, and capacity. The boundary is not unkindness. It is the accounting that makes sustainable kindness possible.

“Carry each other’s burdens… for each one should carry their own load.” — Galatians 6:2, 5
Chapter 3

Set one thing down

Not five things. One. Take the costliest reflex yes from your audit and price the alternative honestly: what does it cost to set it down — a disappointed person, an awkward week, a small guilt — against what it costs to keep carrying it — the sleep, the resentment, the capacity it eats every single week. Most chronic loads survive because the one-time cost of setting them down feels larger than the weekly cost of carrying them. It usually isn’t.

Setting down is not dropping people. It is returning a load to its owner — sometimes with notice, sometimes with a handover, always with the Galatians 6 distinction in hand: if it is crushing them, help carry it. If it is simply theirs, hand it back kindly.

Nehemiah, rebuilding a wall with a real enemy watching, refused the meeting for the fifth time: “I am carrying on a great project and cannot go down.” A holy no, without apology or over-explanation. — Nehemiah 6:3
Artefact — the capacity budget

Two columns for one ordinary week: load out (everything you carry, including the invisible — the organising, the remembering, the emotional management) against recovery in (sleep, rest, being met, play). If out exceeds in three weeks running, something gets set down or something breaks. Those are the only two options the body offers.

Chapter 4

Saying it — the scripts

Most boundaries fail in the mouth, not the heart: the no that arrives wrapped in five justifications invites negotiation, and the no delivered in anger costs the relationship. A clean boundary sentence is short, warm, and complete — it does not argue its case. Borrow these until your own voice takes over; the wording is allowed to be borrowed. That is what scripts are for.

Artefact — the scripts library
  • The plain no: “I can’t take that on.” (Full stop. No appendix.)
  • The warm no: “I love that you thought of me. I don’t have the capacity for it.”
  • The buying-time script: “Let me check my capacity and come back to you tomorrow.” (This one breaks the reflex yes.)
  • The partial yes: “I can do the first part. The rest needs another home.”
  • The setting-down script: “I’ve been carrying this, and I need to hand it back. Here’s what I can help with in the handover.”
  • The need, stated plainly: “What I need is ___.” (Not hinted. Stated.)
  • The repair opener: “I don’t like how that went. Can we try it again?”
  • The limit with love: “I’m not able to talk about this tonight. I want to — tomorrow, when I can actually hear you.”

Expect the sentence to feel worse than it sounds. The discomfort is the protector adjusting, not evidence you were unkind.

Chapter 5

How you reach, how you hide

Underneath the boundary patterns sit attachment patterns — the learned choreography of closeness. In plain language: when connection feels threatened, some of us pursue — reach harder, talk more, close the distance. Some withdraw — go quiet, handle it alone, leave before being left. Some shut down — the conversation continues but nobody is home. And steady is the fourth option: able to reach without panic and to take space without disappearing. The Check-Up asked which one is yours under stress.

Each pattern is protection, learned where it was needed. Pursuit protected a child whose connection was inconsistent. Withdrawal protected one whose closeness cost too much. The work is not to shame the pattern but to widen the options: the pursuer practices one pause before the third text; the withdrawer practices one sentence before the exit — “I’m overwhelmed, not leaving; I need twenty minutes”; the shutdown pattern borrows the Regulation Guide’s coming-back menu. Small, repeated, survived: that is how patterns soften.

Artefact — your reach pattern, in one line

Finish this sentence honestly: “When I feel someone pulling away, I tend to ___, and what I actually need is ___.” The second half is the sentence your people have been waiting to hear.

Chapter 6

Repair and staying

Boundaries get the attention; repair does the long work. Every close relationship ruptures — the question is whether it has a way back. A repair conversation has a shape: name the rupture without prosecuting it, own your specific part (specific — “I went silent for two days,” not “sorry I’m like this”), hear theirs, and agree on the next time. Repair is a corrective experience, not a verdict: it teaches the nervous system that rupture is survivable, which is precisely what the old patterns never learned.

And sometimes the honest outcome is distance — a season of less, or an ending carried with grief rather than contempt. Distance chosen honestly is also a boundary, and it is not a failure of love. Even Jesus withdrew from crowds who wanted more of Him than the day held — and returned. Withdrawing to return is a pattern worth copying; withdrawing to vanish is the one to watch.

“Very early in the morning… Jesus got up, left the house and went off to a solitary place, where he prayed.” The crowds came looking. He had still gone. — Luke 5:16; Mark 1:35
